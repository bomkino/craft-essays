# Craft Essays — foundation for a general writing skill

Research snapshot: 18 September 2026, before implementation of v0.1.0. This document preserves the foundation used to build the skill. References to future packaging and evaluation describe that earlier design stage; see the repository README and evaluation record for release information.

This document supports one ambition: help a writer make better choices across forms, with more perception, substance, precision, expressive range and pleasure. The eventual skill should produce writing or useful editorial judgment when invoked. Its user should not have to study this foundation or conduct a workshop before receiving help.

## Reading this foundation

The decision units below are the authoritative interpretation of the source for this project. The source index establishes coverage; the worked cases examine application; the runtime design and evaluation sections explain how to turn the interpretation into dependable behaviour. These sections serve different purposes and should not all load during an ordinary writing request.

The labels **Source**, **Mechanism**, **Application**, **Boundary** and **Check** distinguish what the essays teach from this project's generalisation. A check describes inspectable textual evidence. It does not establish an actual reader's emotional response.

## The model of craft

Writing develops through a relationship between **material, purpose, form, voice and reader**. Better material can alter the purpose; drafting can reveal a different form; revision can discover what a piece is actually about. This relationship is not a rigid assembly line.

The recurring movement in these essays is from a shortcut to an experience the reader can participate in: a verdict becomes selected evidence; a stock phrase becomes fresh observation; disconnected incidents acquire a shape; an object gathers associations; the writer hears a sentence differently when another person must follow it. The reader helps complete the work.

The techniques also depend on one another. Familiar elements reduce the work of orientation, leaving room for unfamiliar detail. Specific evidence gives the reader something to infer; rhythm gives that inference time. An explicit overview can free attention for a more subtle account. A recurring image can recall earlier feeling without explaining it again. These interactions explain why mechanically maximising one quality—brevity, surprise, specificity or intensity—can weaken the whole.

General writing also needs explicit definitions, conclusions, plain feeling, stable terminology and unambiguous instructions. The skill must judge how much work belongs to the writer and how much can productively belong to the reader. Curiosity, difficulty, silence, beauty and uncertainty can be intended effects. Fast comprehension is one possible aim.

The practical unit of instruction is therefore a conditional decision: **given this passage and its purpose, make this move for this reason; check this result; choose another move when this boundary applies.** Adjectives such as vivid, engaging or powerful are aspirations. They become useful guidance only when connected to a decision an agent can make.

## Decision units

### 1. Authority: earn the reader's trust

**Source:** Establishing Your Authority, pp. 6–9; Submerging the “I”, pp. 38–40; the acknowledged limits of knowledge on p. 105.

**Mechanism:** Knowledge supplies substance; frankness reveals the teller's stake and limits. Together they can make a voice worth attending to. An impressive surface can conceal an absence of either.

**Application:** When a passage asks for trust, find its actual warrant: evidence, relevant experience, reasoning, accountable observation or an openly framed judgment. Put the necessary warrant close enough to the claim to make the relation legible. Prefer a clear, supportable opinion to evasive general approval. Use the author's supplied experience and position accurately.

**Boundary:** Confession is optional. Expertise need not announce itself, and obscurity does not establish it. In factual work, distinguish observation from inference and preserve consequential uncertainty. In an apology, first person can establish responsibility; deleting it may damage the writing. Invented scenes and experiences belong in work where invention is authorised.

**Check:** Can the reader identify why the consequential claim deserves consideration, whose judgment it is, and what remains uncertain? Has an edit made the claim stronger than its support?

### 2. Observation: replace borrowed perception

**Source:** Using “On-the-Body” Physical Sensation, pp. 25–27; Cliches, pp. 66–69; the property descriptions on pp. 64–65; the particular comparisons on p. 151.

**Mechanism:** Stock phrasing can stand in for an experience the writer has not examined. Specific observation gives the reader something to perceive and the writer something to think with. The choice of detail also reveals a point of view.

**Application:** Locate an important label or familiar phrase that does insufficient work. Ask what observation, action, example, relation or consequence would let the reader understand it. Use relevant material already supplied; obtain missing material when the piece depends on it. For an abstract subject, an exact distinction or worked example can provide the needed specificity.

**Boundary:** A precise adjective may already do enough. Familiar expressions can be socially right; specialist terms can be exact; repetition can aid comprehension. Sensory description is valuable when experience matters. Additional imagery can be an expensive distraction in a definition or instruction. Greater specificity must not invent a testimonial, statistic, motive or memory.

**Check:** Does the selected detail explain or distinguish something consequential? Could it fit almost any subject unchanged? If so, the passage may still be generic. If removing it loses nothing useful or expressive, it may be decoration.

### 3. Development: accumulate meaning

**Source:** Developing a Theme, pp. 16–18; Using Your Objects, pp. 98–101; Objects, pp. 120–123; recurring physical conditions and images in the *Fetch* annotations.

**Mechanism:** A limited set of elements can gain meaning through recurrence, variation and changed context. The reader recognises an element without needing to learn a fresh one at every turn. Repetition can develop an idea, provide continuity or establish a rhythm.

**Application:** For an extended piece, identify the central inquiry, proposition or preoccupation. Develop it through a revealing example, complication, counterexample, consequence or change of perspective. Reuse a useful case, term or image when it can carry that development. Select details for what they contribute to the whole.

**Boundary:** Development is not restating the conclusion with different adjectives. Factual inquiry must remain open to evidence that changes the thesis. A many-sided essay may earn several strands. Stable technical terminology often serves better than expressive renaming. A symbol is optional; an email does not need one.

**Check:** On each meaningful return, what has changed in the reader's understanding, feeling or expectation? If nothing changes, does the repetition still earn its place through clarity, rhythm or ritual?

### 4. Orientation: choose what the opening makes available

**Source:** Beware the “Thesis Statement”, pp. 45–48; The Quilt Versus the Big O, pp. 70–74; The Thumbnail, pp. 82–84; The Cycle, especially pp. 89–90.

**Mechanism:** An opening establishes a relationship and a reason to continue. It can disclose an outcome while leaving its causes, significance or experience open. Withholding and orientation have different effects; neither is automatically superior.

**Application:** Decide what the reader needs immediately. For a practical decision or action, usually supply the answer and relevant conditions early. For inquiry, criticism or expressive prose, consider a precise observation, contradiction, question, claim or scene that contains the piece's real interest. Choose a sequence that supports the reader's next question. Revise the opening once the whole piece reveals what it promises.

**Boundary:** The source's sledgehammer opening states the outcome and creates curiosity at once. Its thumbnail form explicitly endorses disclosure. Chronology is often the clearest structure for a procedure or account. A delayed answer must earn the reader's time. A known outcome can deepen interest; a compulsory hook can cheapen a serious piece.

**Check:** Can the reader tell what kind of encounter this is? Does the opening make a promise the rest fulfils or meaningfully revises? Are prerequisites supplied before they are needed?

### 5. Inference: decide what to state and what to let emerge

**Source:** Thesis Statement, pp. 46–48; Effective Similes, pp. 79–81; Disconnected Dialogue, pp. 113–115; Plot Points, pp. 134–136; *Fetch*, pp. 155 and 160.

**Mechanism:** Readers participate when they connect sufficient particulars. Repeating every implication can drain that participation. Conversely, a direct statement can supply essential interpretation, ownership or understanding.

**Application:** Where a passage supplies both an effective demonstration and its obvious paraphrase, test the passage without the paraphrase. Where it offers a verdict without enough grounds, supply evidence or clarify its status. In an explanation, state the relationship that the intended audience would otherwise have to guess. In personal writing, allow a simple declaration of feeling when it carries the truth of the moment.

**Boundary:** Fictional characters can evade each other; a useful reply generally answers the question. Body language is evidence of an action, not certain access to a real person's mind. Deliberate ambiguity in a poem or essay can remain unresolved. Requirements, instructions and consequential qualifications need sufficient explicitness.

**Check:** What can this audience reasonably infer from the actual text? Is a gap rewarding, confusing or evasive? Is an explanation enabling understanding or merely repeating it?

### 6. Embodiment: make experience consequential

**Source:** On-the-Body, pp. 25–27; Punctuation with Gesture and Attribution, pp. 59–60; Body Language, pp. 116–119; cumulative conditions throughout *Fetch*.

**Mechanism:** Experience becomes credible through interacting conditions and their consequences. Gesture can carry meaning, establish who is present, and create a beat of time. A physical condition that disappears when inconvenient weakens continuity.

**Application:** When lived experience matters, use the supplied or authorised details that shape attention and action: what can be heard, reached, endured, noticed or done. In a reported scene, describe observable behaviour with appropriate attribution. In explanation, concrete consequences can make an abstract claim usable. Carry established conditions through subsequent passages where they still matter.

**Boundary:** Intensity is not a universal goal. Quiet pleasure, comfort and stillness have physical texture too. A concise report may need an accurate label more than an enacted scene. Use physical detail for its relevance, not as a bodily checklist. The source's neurological claims are unnecessary to this editorial rationale.

**Check:** Does the detail affect the experience or understanding, rather than merely decorate it? Are actions, sensations and constraints consistent? Is any interpretation being presented as direct observation?

### 7. Rhythm: compose for time and attention

**Source:** Reading Out Loud, pp. 49–58; Punctuation, pp. 59–60; Killing Time, pp. 108–111; Utility Phrases, pp. 127–129.

**Mechanism:** Prose unfolds over time. Syntax, stress, attribution, paragraphing, repetition and silence influence comprehension and feeling. A seemingly light phrase can give a difficult idea, joke or emotional turn room to land.

**Application:** Inspect sentence movement in relation to the thought: where does the main stress fall, where does a reader need a breath, what must remain in memory, and what deserves its own beat? Vary length when the movement benefits. For speech, keep speaker references and sequences legible to a listener who cannot readily look back. Preserve repetition and connective phrases when they contribute cadence, orientation or human ease.

**Boundary:** A fixed short-sentence style reduces range. Long sentences can hold a layered thought or sustained feeling. Hard consonants are one sound resource, not a quality metric. Silent editorial inspection is not an actual performance or audience test; report those separately.

**Check:** Can the intended reader follow the sentence's relationships? Do breaks and stresses support the desired pace? Does compression remove useful breathing space? For performed work, what does an actual read-through reveal?

### 8. Texture: change modes when thought needs them

**Source:** Textures of Information, pp. 75–78; Thirteen Writing Tips, p. 105; Killing Time, pp. 109–111; the shift between scene and reflection on p. 150.

**Mechanism:** Examples, explanation, quotations, instructions, lists, reflection and direct address ask the reader to do different things. A change of mode can clarify, reorient, vary pace or extend a thought.

**Application:** When prose becomes monotonous or abstract, identify the missing mode. An argument may need an example; a list may need interpretation; a scene may need reflective distance; a dense explanation may need a worked case. Introduce the mode that does that work, then return with a clear connection.

**Boundary:** Variety has a switching cost. A short message may need one mode only. Formatting something like an official report does not make it evidence. A quotation needs genuine attribution. A digression's interest alone does not establish that it belongs.

**Check:** What does the switch make clearer, more felt or more interesting? Can the reader connect the inserted material to the piece? Does the chosen form carry any misleading suggestion of authority?

### 9. Analogy: reveal a precise relationship

**Source:** Effective Similes, pp. 79–81; *Fetch* comparisons and commentary, pp. 144 and 151–152.

**Mechanism:** Comparison transfers attention and associations. It can reveal a relationship quickly or produce a valuable imaginative experience. It also makes the reader hold another object or world in mind.

**Application:** Establish what is being compared and the exact shared property, action or relation. Develop that connection far enough to help. For explanatory analogy, make its limit clear where misunderstanding would matter. For expressive prose, assess the image's aptness, sound and relation to the writer's world. Prefer the best coherent comparison over a succession of unrelated images.

**Boundary:** The source both restricts comparisons and experiments with extending them. The operative question is their effect. Literal precision may work best. An unusual metaphor is not automatically perceptive, and a vivid one cannot prove a factual claim.

**Check:** Can the intended connection be named? Does the comparison clarify or enrich the subject more than it distracts? Has the analogy silently imported a false relationship?

### 10. Voice: write from a situated intelligence

**Source:** Submerging the “I”, pp. 38–40; Cliches, pp. 66–69; Thirteen Writing Tips, pp. 103–105; Names Versus Pronouns, pp. 130–133; vocabulary and perception in *Fetch*, pp. 148, 151 and 155.

**Mechanism:** Voice emerges from attention, knowledge, values, diction, syntax and relationship. It is expressed in what a writer notices and chooses to say. It can include roughness, restraint, warmth, anger, play or uncertainty.

**Application:** Read the user's supplied prose for what is already alive. Preserve characteristic commitments, rhythms and useful irregularities while addressing the requested problem. For new writing, use the brief and intended relationship to establish a suitable register. Let opinions, jokes and tenderness arise during composition when the material invites them. Use the author's chosen names, pronouns and terminology consistently.

**Boundary:** Learning a mechanism does not require reproducing Palahniuk's verbal signatures. Local slang is not a universal upgrade. Stylistic standardisation can erase a person. The skill should serve a restrained scientist, exuberant friend, careful critic or lyrical essayist without imposing a house persona. Context can require plain “I” or unshowy familiar language.

**Check:** Does the revision still sound plausibly like the intended writer addressing this reader? Which concrete choices produce that effect? Have cleverness, polish or compression displaced the writer's stake?

### 11. Surprise and recognition: give the reader something worth meeting

**Source:** Required Reading: Absurdity, pp. 124–126; Thirteen Writing Tips, pp. 102–107; Plot Points, pp. 135–136; the social dynamics of Disconnected Dialogue, pp. 113–115.

**Mechanism:** A noticed incongruity, unexpected connection or accurately named private experience can bring pleasure and recognition. Surprise works in relation to an established expectation. Familiar forms can make unusual material accessible.

**Application:** Look for the particular friction already present: a mismatch between a claim and a behaviour, a system and its purpose, a ritual and its consequences. Let that observation shape a joke, image or judgment. In expressive work, permit a distinctive angle and emotional risk. In analytical work, develop the implication that makes the subject worth examining.

**Boundary:** Humour is optional and relationship-dependent. Absurdity may be appropriate to satire but inaccurate in reporting. A serious letter need not become entertaining. An ending can leave a resonant question rather than deliver a maxim. Novelty in every sentence can exhaust the reader.

**Check:** What makes this observation particular to this subject? Does its surprise reward attention or seek attention for itself? Is the ending stronger before the extra explanation or flourish?

### 12. Revision: work on the level where the weakness lives

**Source:** Read-aloud revision, pp. 49–52; Cliches, p. 67; Thirteen Writing Tips, pp. 103–105; Plot Points, pp. 134–135; *Fetch*, pp. 140, 148, 153 and 159–160.

**Mechanism:** Completing a workable sequence and deepening its meaning are different achievements. A local defect may wait while the whole takes shape. Later reflection can reveal an insufficient premise, an easy turn, undeveloped feeling or a familiar device used without fresh purpose.

**Application:** Match the intervention to the weakness: gather material, sharpen the thought, restructure, develop an implication, alter emphasis, rewrite a sentence, or leave a successful passage alone. For substantial work, settle the whole sufficiently before polishing every local surface. Then revisit meaning and experience as well as syntax. Return to the brief after revision to catch drift.

**Boundary:** Drafting need not enforce every technique at once. The user may request proofreading, a diagnosis, an experiment or a finished rewrite; honour that scope. A small request should not trigger an elaborate visible process. More editing is not proof of greater care.

**Check:** What specific weakness did the revision address, and what textual change supports the claimed improvement? What was valuable before and is it still present? Are the piece's remaining problems named honestly?

## Tensions that must survive distillation

These pairs are part of the knowledge, not inconsistencies to erase when shortening the skill.

| Decision | Prefer one move when… | Prefer the other when… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct statement / accumulated particulars | The reader needs an answer, definition, ownership or plain feeling. | Discovery, sensation or an earned interpretation is part of the piece's value. |
| Concision / expansion | Detail repeats or distracts; the reader needs quick action. | A thought, feeling or experience needs development or room. |
| Familiarity / surprise | Shared language, ritual, a technical term or a known form helps. | Received phrasing hides the subject's particularity or a fresh connection. |
| Repetition / variation | Reference, rhythm or a developing motif benefits from recognition. | A recurring element has stopped contributing or a new mode is needed. |
| Explanation / inference | The connection would otherwise be unclear or consequentially misleading. | The reader has enough to make a rewarding connection. |
| Smoothness / productive roughness | Friction obstructs the intended reading. | A hesitation, fragment, unusual syntax or difficulty carries voice and meaning. |
| Planning / discovery | Dependencies, a brief or a known argument require order. | Writing is still finding the subject; the next move can be known without a final conclusion. |
| Confidence / qualification | Evidence and judgment support a firm statement. | The claim's scope, uncertainty or limits affect its meaning. |
| Closure / openness | A promised answer, practical request or argument needs fulfilment. | A poem, reflection or question gains meaning by remaining open. |

The user's purpose and constraints govern the choice. When two effects matter, preserve both where possible before declaring a trade-off inevitable.

## The essays as demonstrations of nonfiction

Three close readings prevent the foundation from becoming a list of tips detached from writing.

**The sledgehammer, pp. 45–48.** The opening announces an obligation that makes no practical sense. The following list slows the reader through the objects, their cost and their burden. The explanation connects that experience to optional writing tools. The opening is already a direct statement; what matters is the question inside it. The passage combines declaration, accumulation and interpretation. Its lesson cannot be faithfully reduced to banning thesis sentences.

**The plum tree, pp. 64–65.** The seller selects a tree's history; the agent selects dimensions and specifications. Their descriptions reveal different relations to the property. For a general writing skill, the decisive question is which knowledge the reader needs and which relation the piece wants to establish. The tree is rich material for an account of attachment; specifications may be decisive in a purchase. Turning the anecdote into a ban on technical detail would lose the mechanism.

**The unfinished draft, pp. 140–160.** The annotations expose choices, repeated words deferred for repair, a reused device questioned, and an easy resolution identified for development. The proposed ending is not proof that the final published story used it. The general lesson is the ability to notice the level on which a draft is incomplete. A functioning sequence can still lack emotional or intellectual depth. The introductory beard anecdote also acknowledges the cost of excessive self-monitoring while composing.

## Proposed runtime design

This is a design to implement and evaluate later. It is not an installed instruction set.

### The common path

Apply the skill to the writing assignment and material in the current context. If an invocation supplies neither and the context does not resolve the task, ask what the user wants to write or revise. A bare invocation should lead to the missing brief, not an unsolicited craft lecture. Carry the selected approach through revisions of that assignment; do not turn it into a global persona or alter the user's settings.

1. **Establish the assignment.** Read the request and supplied material. Identify the intended reader, occasion, requested operation, facts, format and voice worth preserving. Infer what is clear; ask only for missing information that materially changes the result. Completion: the agent can distinguish what should change from what is protected. For a small request, this can remain a brief internal judgment.
2. **Find the leverage.** Locate the most consequential weakness or unrealised opportunity: material, thought, structure, emphasis, expression, rhythm or voice. Identify something already working. Completion: a specific passage or unmet need supports the diagnosis; “make it better” alone does not.
3. **Select and compose.** Use the relevant decision units, including their boundaries. Draft, revise or critique according to the requested operation. For substantial exploratory work, permit the diagnosis to change as the piece develops. Completion: the requested work exists and addresses the diagnosed need; the agent has not merely given advice about doing it.
4. **Compare and deepen.** Review the result against the brief, protected material and diagnosis. Check claims and implications, then inspect the selected craft effects. Preserve or restore valuable qualities lost in revision. Completion: consequential failures found in this review are resolved, or clearly identified when essential information is unavailable.
5. **Deliver appropriately.** Return the requested writing or critique in the requested form. Add concise editorial rationale when requested or needed to explain a material choice. Completion: the output is usable without the agent's private working notes. An already successful passage may remain unchanged.

### Disclosure that earns its cost

The entrypoint should carry the common path and the essential preservation rules. References should carry the conditional decisions, applications and counterexamples. Group references by decisions an agent actually needs to make, not automatically into 31 essay files or a separate skill for every genre.

Provisional routing:

- Questions about focus, order, development or endings reach the structure material: units 3–5 and the relevant part of 12.
- Generic claims, thin observation, weak authority or comparison reach the substance material: units 1–2, 6 and 9.
- Voice, cadence, humour, emotional expression or monotony reach the expression material: units 7–8 and 10–11.

A pointer must name the condition and what its target helps decide. Essential boundaries travel with each decision unit. A skill should not load every reference simply because all writing has structure and voice.

The final file boundaries remain provisional until runtime work shows what agents actually need together. The source index and evaluation rationale are maintainer material. Runtime writing should not require reading them.

### Scale and portability

Drafting, revising, critiquing and proofreading are distinct requested operations. The same user may move between them; the skill should follow that scope without demanding another skill invocation. Make only changes authorised by the current request. Exact quotations, figures, names, commitments, interface labels and deliberately locked wording retain their meaning and required form.

Use ordinary files and relative references for the future package. Keep source attribution separate from runtime prerequisites. Installation should not depend on private studio material, local absolute paths, an account connection or another uninstalled writing skill. Verify host metadata and invocation controls when packaging; the explicit `$craft-essays` spelling is currently a proposed name, not a tested installation claim.

The current material and worked cases are English. Sentence music, conventions and register must be interpreted in the user's language; multilingual behaviour requires its own review. “All sorts of writing” describes breadth of intent, not a measured guarantee for every language and domain.

## Worked transfer cases

All factual packets and drafts below are synthetic. They are original exercises, not real messages, testimonials or empirical findings. The candidate wording is one defensible response, not a golden string that every future output must match. These are desk-reviewed design cases; an installed skill has not been run against them.

### A. An accountable apology

**Brief and facts:** Improve a short apology. The writer missed Thursday's deadline, delaying the recipient's decision. The revised estimate is committed for Friday at 3 p.m.

**Draft:** “Unfortunately, there has been a delay in the delivery of the estimate.”

**Candidate:** “I missed Thursday's deadline. I'll send the revised estimate by 3 p.m. Friday. I'm sorry for holding up your decision.”

**Decision:** Units 1, 4 and 10. Ownership, timing and acknowledgment matter. Keeping “I” is the right choice; the commitment belongs early. **Failure to catch:** an impersonal rewrite, invented excuses, a different deadline, or emotional theatre that burdens the recipient.

### B. An explanation with an exact relationship

**Brief and facts:** Explain two controls to a new user. Save stores a draft. Publish makes it visible to other people. Saving alone does not publish.

**Draft:** “Save and Publish provide a seamless pathway for managing your content experience.”

**Candidate:** “Save keeps your work as a draft. Publish makes it visible to other people. You can save your changes before you're ready to publish them.”

**Decision:** Units 2, 4 and 5. The abstraction is replaced with the relationship the user needs. **Failure to catch:** a vivid but inaccurate analogy, invented autosave, or changing the labels to avoid repetition.

### C. The same event, two valid forms

**Facts:** A free repair session takes place Thursday, 6–8 p.m., at the community hall. Bring one garment. Beginners are welcome. Needles, thread and a sewing machine are available; volunteers help.

**Warm invitation:** “Bring the shirt you keep meaning to mend. There'll be needles, thread, a sewing machine, and someone to help. Thursday, 6–8 p.m., at the community hall. Free; beginners welcome. Please bring one garment.”

**Notice:** “Free clothing repair session: Thursday, 6–8 p.m., at the community hall. Bring one garment; beginners are welcome. Volunteers will help, and needles, thread and a sewing machine will be available.”

**Decision:** Units 2, 4 and 10. The invitation begins with a recognisable situation; the notice begins with identification and logistics. Both fit their briefs. **Failure to catch:** treating one as the universal better style or promising that every garment will be repaired.

### D. A claim that needs a narrower conclusion

**Brief and facts:** Improve an internal recommendation. In a survey of twelve self-selected trial participants, eight preferred the four-day schedule and four did not. The proposed next step is a larger trial.

**Draft:** “The results prove that everyone wants a four-day week, so we should move ahead.”

**Candidate:** “Eight of the twelve volunteers preferred the four-day schedule. That supports running a larger trial; it does not establish what the rest of the staff would choose.”

**Decision:** Units 1, 3 and 5. Specific evidence strengthens the recommendation by establishing its actual scope. **Failure to catch:** replacing false certainty with vague enthusiasm, losing the self-selection limit, or inventing productivity gains.

### E. A review with an arguable judgment

**Brief and observations:** Write two sentences of concert criticism. Earlier choruses had the full band; the final chorus was sung alone. Ten seconds passed after the last word before applause began. The critic found that restraint effective.

**Weak draft:** “The performance was incredibly moving and utterly unforgettable.”

**Candidate:** “The final chorus leaves the singer alone after the full band has carried every earlier return. Ten seconds pass before the applause: the restraint works.”

**Decision:** Units 1, 2 and 11. The reader can consider a particular judgment and its basis. **Failure to catch:** claiming certain knowledge of the audience's emotions or merely upgrading the superlatives. Another critic could reasonably interpret the silence differently.

### F. A spoken appeal with room to land

**Brief and facts:** Revise a short speech asking a committee to keep a library open on Saturdays. Forty-two people used it last Saturday: children doing homework and adults completing applications. No other statistics are supplied.

**Draft:** “Saturday opening is vital to the community and creates meaningful access to essential opportunities for everyone.”

**Candidate:** “Forty-two people used the library last Saturday. Children did their homework. Adults filled in applications. Please keep it open on Saturdays.”

**Decision:** Units 2, 4 and 7. The evidence supports a direct appeal; separate beats suit this brief. **Failure to catch:** invented hardship, unsupported universal claims, or assuming that all speeches should use this staccato cadence. The candidate still needs an actual spoken read-through before any performance claim.

### G. Personal thanks in plain language

**Brief and facts:** Thank a friend who stayed on the phone while the writer revised an application. The writer needed the company.

**Candidate:** “You stayed on the phone while I rewrote the last paragraph. I needed the company. Thank you.”

**Decision:** Units 5, 7 and 10. A specific acknowledgment supports a direct feeling. The familiar phrase is apt. **Failure to catch:** compulsory novelty, an invented shared memory, or a grand metaphor that makes the gratitude sound performed.

### H. Preserve deliberate ambiguity

**Brief:** Lightly edit this short poem without explaining its situation.

**Draft:** “Your mug, / handle turned / toward the empty chair.”

**Candidate:** Preserve the words and line breaks unless the user supplies a more specific concern.

**Decision:** Units 5, 10 and 12. The object and orientation already permit several readings. **Failure to catch:** adding a death, breakup or moral; replacing ambiguity with a declared emotion; converting the poem into conventional prose. No edit is a legitimate result.

### I. Protect a sustained sentence

**Brief:** Check a lyrical sentence for clarity while preserving its unhurried rhythm.

**Draft:** “All afternoon the curtain lifted from the open window, settled against the sill, and lifted again, while the room waited for the heat to leave.”

**Candidate:** Keep the sentence.

**Decision:** Units 3, 7 and 10. Its repetition and personification sustain the intended stillness. **Failure to catch:** compressing it into “The curtain moved all afternoon” or splitting every clause to satisfy a short-sentence preference. Its value depends on the requested register, not literal informational efficiency.

### J. Humour from the material

**Brief and facts:** Write a playful internal sign. The downstairs printer has jammed after taking three sheets; the upstairs printer is available. A lightly absurd personification is welcome.

**Candidate:** “The downstairs printer has eaten three sheets and would like a fourth. Please use the printer upstairs.”

**Decision:** Units 2, 4 and 11. The joke develops the actual malfunction and leaves the practical action clear. **Failure to catch:** a detached punchline, an invented technical diagnosis, or carrying this register into a serious incident notice.

### K. Exact scope in a proofreading request

**Brief:** Correct spelling only. Preserve the deliberately fragmentary voice and all numbers.

**Draft:** “Three rooms. Two windows. One very stubborn draught. We seperate the work into 4 stages.”

**Candidate:** “Three rooms. Two windows. One very stubborn draught. We separate the work into 4 stages.”

**Decision:** Unit 12 and the assignment boundary. **Failure to catch:** expanding the fragments, changing “4” to “four,” Americanising “draught,” or replacing the writer's comic emphasis. Craft includes respecting the requested intervention.

### L. Expand an underdeveloped reflection

**Brief and material:** Develop an essay paragraph from the unused-tool anecdote in the provided source. Keep the factual basis in that anecdote and identify the analogy as interpretation.

**Thin draft:** “Palahniuk's story shows why writing advice should be optional.”

**Candidate:** “An unused tool is easy to defend: one day, you might need it. Palahniuk makes us reckon with what happens until that day arrives. The tools cost money he had to borrow; their weight followed him from the car to his station. Advice can acquire the same exemption from scrutiny. A rule kept for possible use can become a rule enforced on every sentence.”

**Decision:** Units 3, 8, 9 and 12. Expansion supplies the reasoning connecting an anecdote to a claim. **Failure to catch:** adding only decorative language, mistaking the analogy for empirical proof, or compressing away the development because shorter is assumed better.

## What would establish that the skill helps?

The worked cases establish design intent and expose predictable misuse. They do not measure improvement from a skill that does not yet exist.

### Evaluation after implementation

Use the same model, task material, constraints and generation settings, with comparable output budgets, for runs with and without the candidate skill. Use fresh contexts: the baseline should not inherit this study, and the candidate should receive only the material an actual installation would expose. Keep construction examples separate from previously unseen evaluation briefs. Retain the actual outputs and inputs. Include drafting, revision, critique, narrow edits and cases where restraint is right; include both practical and expressive forms.

First inspect assignment and factual integrity. A changed number, fabricated source, lost caveat, erased voice or scope violation is a material failure even if the prose becomes attractive. Use simple mechanical checks only for exact constraints that benefit from them. Word counts and prohibited-word scans cannot establish writing quality.

Then compare anonymised, shuffled pairs against the actual brief. Ask a reader to identify preferences with textual reasons: the thought became more developed, a distinction became clearer, the voice survived, an image became apt, the rhythm supported the occasion, or a needed answer became easier to find. Permit a tie or “both fail.” Report dimensions separately when one version gains something and loses something else. Use human judgment for any public claim about readers' taste; model preferences are labelled as such.

Probe the decisions that could otherwise become slogans:

- A direct opening beside a case that benefits from delayed interpretation.
- A precise short message beside a passage that needs expansion.
- Useful repetition beside monotonous repetition.
- A plain emotional statement beside an unsupported emotional label.
- A distinctive rough voice beside accidentally confusing syntax.
- A supplied fact beside missing evidence that cannot honestly be manufactured.
- An explanation that needs explicitness beside a poem that needs ambiguity.
- A playful brief beside a serious one using similar subject matter.

For an important instruction whose contribution is uncertain, compare a candidate with that instruction removed. Retain it only when the evidence or a concrete failure shows why it matters. This applies writing-for-agents' test for instructions that merely restate the model's default behaviour.

After a revision to the skill, rerun the affected cases and a small varied regression selection. Broaden testing when new failures or broader changes justify it. A new skill should not claim universal quality gains from a handful of agreeable samples.

### Completion criteria for this foundation

- Every titled entry has a source range and a disposition: covered in the index above.
- Every decision unit has a mechanism, application, boundary and inspectable check: twelve units completed.
- The source's consequential tensions remain visible: nine paired decisions recorded.
- General-writing transfer is concrete: twelve worked cases include useful edits, stylistic alternatives, expansion and restraint.
- Runtime and evaluation design are separated from source exposition: specified above.
- Original source, interpretation, synthetic examples and unmeasured hypotheses are distinguished throughout.

The next phase can implement a compact entrypoint and the smallest reference structure that preserves these decisions. Then its behaviour can be evaluated. Publication and installation claims require that later work; this foundation makes no such claim.
