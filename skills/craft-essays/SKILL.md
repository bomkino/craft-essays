# Craft Essays

Great writing starts from what the piece is for. Know who will read it and what should change in them. Then choose the particulars, order them, and let the reader arrive one step before the words do.

The tools below come from Chuck Palahniuk's craft essays and the teachers he credits. They are tools, not a style: the finished piece sounds like its author and its occasion.

## Steps

1. **Find the purpose.** Name the reader and what should change in them once they have read it: what they understand, feel, believe, decide or do. Name what the writer wants and what the occasion allows. List what must survive untouched: facts, figures, names, quotations, commitments, exact terms, the requested form and length, and the author's voice with its useful roughness. When another skill hands you a draft, its brief, voice and markers govern; return the draft in the same shape. Done when you can say in one sentence what the piece must do, and for whom. Scale every later step to the piece: a two-line note takes a glance, a deck takes all of them.
2. **Plan the piece.** Choose the shape the purpose needs; the branch reference for the medium lists the options. Pick the two to four horses the piece will ride: the ideas, images, objects or phrases it keeps returning to. Give every unit (paragraph, slide, section, scene) one job: orient, prove, reveal, set up, pay off, pause or ask. On a craft pass, find the horses and jobs already in the draft. Done when each unit has a job and no two share one.
3. **Write.** Draft or revise with the tools the purpose calls for. Build the horizontal first, the chain of events or points in order; then the vertical, the feeling or conviction that accumulates toward the end. Done when the whole sequence exists.
4. **Test against the plain version.** For each crafted choice (an image, a withheld point, a returning line, a turn of phrase, a change of form), picture the plain version. Keep whichever does more for this reader: clearer, truer, more felt, more memorable, more pleasure to read. A device that does nothing the plain version doesn't is mannerism; a flat sentence where the piece needed life is a missed chance.
5. **Run the revision passes** over everything you wrote or changed.
6. **Deliver** the work in the requested form, finished: no `??????` left in it. Add a note only when asked, or in one line after the work when a choice changed meaning or a missing fact would make the piece stronger.

## Tools

Choose each tool for the purpose. When a rule works against the piece, the piece wins.

- **Harvest.** The best material is often already in the notes: the author's asides, their own phrases, the detail they light up about. Use their words where they are good; those words carry the voice as well as the facts. Rewriting everything into your own register is how a piece stops sounding like its author.
- **Received text.** Your first phrasing is usually the one the language hands everybody. Swap it for what this subject actually does, in words this speaker would use. A familiar phrase stays when it is exact, protected, or the socially right thing to say.
- **Unpack.** A label (seamless, difficult, nervous, world-class, a lot) asks the reader to take your word. Replace it with the particulars that earned it: the action, figure, object, sound or consequence. Draw them from the material. In a draft, mark a missing one `??????` and keep going; in finished work, narrow the claim to what the material supports and name the missing fact after the work. Unpack at the point of meaning: the one or two particulars that carry the claim.
- **The reader arrives first.** Order the particulars so the reader reaches the conclusion a beat before the sentence that states it. Then cut that sentence, or keep it short when this reader needs it confirmed. A demonstration followed by its explanation is the commonest waste in prose.
- **Thumbnail.** When the reader must decide or act, give the whole answer up front: the decision, date, amount, ask, or the arc in one breath. Then unpack. Practical writing, decks and summaries open this way, and it tells readers their time will be well spent. Where the reader must act, thumbnail; where they must feel or believe, let them arrive first. Most substantial pieces do both.
- **Horses.** Ride the same horses from the first line to the last. Each return carries new weight: fresh context, higher stakes, a changed owner. A new example on every page makes a slide show; the same horses returning changed make a story.
- **Buried gun.** Plant early what the ending will need: a detail, figure, promise, question or object. Let the reader half-forget it, then fire it to force the crisis or resolve it. Every payoff was planted; every plant pays off or leaves.
- **Big voice, little voice.** Little voice is the concrete: action, object, figure, quotation, scene. Big voice is the general claim: headline, thesis, reflection, moral. Earn every big-voice line with little-voice material beside it, and keep the mix heavily little. A big-voice line can also pause a scene and imply time passing.
- **Head and heart.** Earn trust through knowledge (the right term, the specific process, the detail only a practitioner notices) or through candour (a limit, a cost, a mistake, a stake). Place it early, where trust is first asked for. Cleverness for its own sake, charm, and telling readers what to feel spend trust instead of earning it.
- **Submerge the I.** Keep the camera on the subject and the reader. Bring in "I" and "we" where they carry responsibility, testimony or a decision.
- **Textures.** When one mode has run long, change it: a list, a figure, an instruction, a definition, a quotation, a scene, a question. Real-world forms (recipes, rules, labels, directions) lend their authority when the material is real.
- **Gradual reveal.** Release information in steps, each clear and satisfying, each raising the next question. Pace the steps by the reader's appetite: the next reveal arrives just as they start to want it.
- **Read it aloud.** Timing is most of it. Read consequential work aloud and fix the exact stumble: the breath that runs out, the unclear speaker, the laugh with no room, the turn that comes too soon. Say when a read was silent.

## Revision passes

The essays taught with time-boxed bans: six months without clichés, a month without pronouns. Here each ban becomes a pass: find every instance, then decide each one. Run every pass over the whole piece; on a narrow edit, run the passes that touch what changed.

1. **Labels.** Mark each abstract adjective, feeling word and unsupported claim. Unpack it, keep it (exact, protected, or the answer the reader needs), or cut it.
2. **Explanations.** Check the sentence after each demonstration and the last line of each section. Cut restated points, closing morals and summaries of what the reader already holds.
3. **Received text.** Mark every phrase whose shape you could predict before reading it, including the rhythms of generated prose: matched triplets, mirrored contrasts, a question answered by its own next line, uplifting closers. Replace each with this subject's particulars, or cut it.
4. **Thought verbs.** In stories, scenes and persuasion, mark knew, realised, felt, understood, believed, wanted and remembered. Replace each with what the person does, notices or says that lets the reader know. Keep the verb where uncertainty, testimony or consent is the point.
5. **Horses and guns.** List every recurring element. Each return changes something; each payoff was planted; one-off elements that do no work go.
6. **Jobs.** Read unit by unit: one job each, in the right order, each arriving when the reader needs it.
7. **Sound.** Read aloud, or mark the read as silent. For line work, read [sentences](references/sentences.md).
8. **Truth and completeness.** Compare against the material. Every fact, figure (including any you calculate), quotation, outcome, feeling and piece of biography traces to the material or to invention the brief allows. Every fact the reader needs is present. Protected wording is intact. Form and length are met. When cutting takes the piece under a required length, the length wins: slow down a moment the material already holds, or give a detail its full weight, and add no new events. A note explaining a missed length is no substitute. No `??????` remains in finished work. Opinion, observation and clearly figurative language are the writer's to make.

Done when all eight passes have run, every mark has a decision, and the piece does what step 1 said it must.
