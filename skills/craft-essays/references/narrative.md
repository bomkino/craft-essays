# Narrative

Fiction, scenes, treatments, personal essays, memoir, and the true stories inside case studies and decks. In fiction, invent inside the premise. In true stories, every particular comes from the material.

## Shape

Pick the shape that serves the story. The straight line of "and then, and then" is the weakest one.

- **Big O.** Open at the crisis, drop back to how it began, return to the crisis changed. It gives a gripping first page, a promise of where the story ends, and a visible teller.
- **Thumbnail (overture).** Give the whole arc up front, then tell it well enough that the reader forgets they know the ending. Write it last, ground it in one small physical scene, and hint lightly.
- **Quilt.** An envelope situation holds several shorter stories joined by a shared question: who will win, who will get out. Decide whether the envelope or the stories are stronger, and let that one win.
- **Cycle.** The trap that runs again: a sanctuary that looks too good, denial, the first losses, the trap closing, proof too late, a last escape attempt, the next arrival. Open by seducing the reader the way the trap seduces its victim.
- **Rebel, follower, witness.** Two answers to a system, both destroyed by it, and the witness who leaves changed. Use it as a lens on choices; real people keep their complexity.
- **Myth as blueprint.** Name the old story underneath (quest, bargain with the devil, trap, rescue) and study the original. Strange settings land when the shape is familiar.
- **Punk song.** For short pieces: start in motion, go fast, get out.

## Scenes

- **Open where something is already happening,** and let the setting appear as someone moves through it.
- **On the body.** Make a sensation happen in the reader by unpacking the physical event moment by moment: pressure, temperature, texture, sound, the inside of the mouth, the soles of the feet. The reader supplies the name of the feeling. Keep accumulated conditions (wet clothes, a cut hand, the weight carried) present until the story resolves them.
- **Thought verbs.** Where a character knew, realised, felt or wanted, show what they do, notice or say that lets the reader know it. Keep the verb when doubt or testimony is the point.
- **Gesture before dialogue.** When a gesture can carry the beat, use the gesture. Let the body contradict the words.
- **Dialogue that talks past itself.** Answer a question with the speaker's preoccupation instead of the information. Let questions hang; the reader knows the answer. Attribute lines with "says", which readers skim, so the quotation lands harder. Pace a long exchange with physical business: one step in a task that runs the length of the scene.
- **Power moves.** In every scene someone gains power, loses it or takes it back.
- **Objects.** Keep few. Recycle them, pass them hand to hand, and let each appearance change what they mean: a joke, then a gift, then a betrayal. An object on the page gets used.
- **The lie as a gun.** A secret, a crime or a promise nobody means to keep, planted early, drives the story until it can no longer be kept. Unmask it before the reader tires.

## Perception and voice

- **Submerge the I.** In first person, keep the camera on the world. Let the narrator surface late, through "my" and "me" before "I".
- **Characters reveal themselves by how they describe the world.** They name things specifically (the good scissors, the neighbour's broken gate), draw comparisons from their own lives, and count time and distance in their own units: bus stops, rinse cycles, songs on the drive.
- **Use the narrator's vocabulary.** A word the narrator would never know makes the prose sound like its author.
- **Idiom.** Give each character their own way of saying things, including the slightly wrong phrase that makes readers slow down and hear a person.
- **Comedy and absurdity** come from specifics: treat a mundane task too seriously or a serious one too lightly, and keep every detail concrete enough to picture.
- **Names.** People meet a stranger by look and action first, then by relationship, and learn the name last. Label characters the same way, change labels as identity shifts, and keep every referent clear.
- **Big voice.** A cutaway from the scene to a general observation changes texture, implies time passing and gives the reader a breath. Use it after the scene has earned it.

## Time

Imply elapsed time with linked action, a cutaway, a recap of accumulated conditions, parallel plotlines entered later each time, or an information dump: a tight, self-contained aside of facts within the character's knowledge, offered without explaining how they know. A plain "Two hours later" is right when speed matters most.

## True stories

- Case studies, memoir and personal essays build scenes only from what happened. Action, quotation, feeling and outcome all come from the material.
- Head: the specific decisions and why they were made. Heart: the limit, the mixed result, the thing that failed. The candid part makes the rest believable.
- End on the last true particular. A moral the reader could write themselves can go.

## Draft, then deepen

Build the horizontal first: the chain of events. Deepen the vertical in revision: the feeling that accumulates toward a change. When the ending resists, reread the early pages for a dropped detail to bring back as the gun.
