# Structure: a sequence the reader can use

## Open the right question

Decide what the reader needs immediately. A decision memo, answer or instruction usually benefits from the conclusion or action up front. A reflective piece may begin with a particular observation, contradiction or scene. Choose the opening that contains the actual interest of the piece.

Disclosure can create curiosity. Knowing the result leaves room to explore why it happened, what it cost or what it means. A summary can orient readers for subtler work later. If delaying an answer only makes a practical task harder, supply it sooner.

Check the opening again after drafting: what does it promise, and does the piece deliver? A punchy hook that leads somewhere else is a weak opening. Chronology, a direct claim and a quiet beginning are all available choices.

## Develop rather than restate

Find the piece's central inquiry or concern. Each substantial return should add an example, implication, complication, counterexample or change of perspective. Repeating the same conclusion in fresh adjectives supplies little development.

Use a limited set of useful elements. A recurring example lets readers learn the subject more deeply without constantly learning a new situation. An object or image can accumulate feeling as its context changes. Stable technical terms can provide the same continuity. Keep factual inquiry open to evidence that changes the thesis.

For each passage, identify what it contributes. The contribution may be understanding, atmosphere, uncertainty, intimacy, rhythm or a needed pause. It need not be a new fact or plot event. Cut or reshape passages whose apparent purpose is already fulfilled elsewhere, unless the recurrence itself matters.

## Allocate the work of understanding

When details already let the reader make a satisfying connection, try removing the obvious explanation that follows. When the connection would be unclear to this audience, state it. When a verdict has no grounds, develop those grounds or identify it honestly as an impression.

Plain feeling can be exact: “I needed the company. Thank you.” A procedure should name its action. A poem may leave its situation open. Judge whether a gap invites participation, causes confusion or avoids answering.

## End at the right depth

Resolve the practical request or the promise an argument makes. For reflective and expressive work, consider what image, implication or question can remain with the reader. Test the ending before its extra summary: a moral can repeat what the piece has already made available.

During revision, distinguish a complete sequence from a developed piece. A draft can proceed coherently and still leave its best thought or feeling unexplored. Expand that part when it matters; shortening is only one form of improvement.

**Decision check:** What does the reader know, feel or expect at each turn? Is the next passage needed there? Does the ending grow from what came before?
