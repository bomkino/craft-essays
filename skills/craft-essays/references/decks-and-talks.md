# Decks and talks

Decks, pitch documents, presentations of work, conference talks and speeches. A deck gets read in a room, skimmed in an inbox and remembered as two or three things. A talk exists only in time. Write both for the moment a page turns or a line is heard.

## The whole deck

- **Thumbnail it.** Say what this is, what you are asking for and why now, on the first pages or in the first minute. A reader who knows where the deck is going relaxes into it, and every later page can unpack instead of withholding.
- **Or open on the moment.** A story deck or a creative presentation can open on its most charged point (the problem at its worst, the scene that sells the story), drop back to how it started, and return to that moment at the close with new meaning. This is the Big O. Either way, the ask gets a page of its own.
- **Two to four horses.** Choose the ideas, images or phrases every page pulls with. Each returns changed: first as the problem, then as evidence, then as the reason to say yes. A new idea per slide makes a slide show.
- **One job per page.** Write the job before the page: orient, prove, reveal, set up, pay off, pause or ask. Two pages with one job merge.
- **Bury a gun.** Plant the detail the close will fire: a figure, a person, an object, a question. An ask that pays off something planted ten pages earlier feels inevitable.
- **Own the weakness.** Raise the problem the audience will certainly notice before they do, once, with what you will do about it, where it matters most. Candour offered early is heart authority; the same fact extracted in questions costs the whole deck.

## Every page

- **Headline in big voice, body in little voice.** The headline makes one plain claim. The body proves it with particulars: a figure, a quotation, an image, a step in a process, a moment. The body resolves the headline instead of repeating it.
- **Write headlines a sceptic would sign.** "Strong community impact" is a label. The count of people served last month, taken from the material, is a particular, and it can be the headline.
- **One thing per page, done well.** On-slide copy holds what must be seen; the speaker note or the next page holds the rest. A deck read alone carries more words than one projected and spoken over.
- **Change textures when a form has run long:** a figure, a quotation, a timeline, a map, a single image with a caption, a list, a short scene. Every quotation and testimonial is real and approved; where one is missing, the page works without it and the gap goes on the list for the author.

## Story decks and treatments

Film, television and advertising decks sell a story before it exists. Everything in [narrative](narrative.md) applies, plus:

- **The logline is the thumbnail.** One sentence that makes this story's distinctive tension or experience graspable. For a pursuit story that is often a person, what they want, what stands in the way and what it costs; a relationship, a ritual, an inquiry or a formal experiment finds its own sentence. Adjectives about the story (gripping, heartfelt, unforgettable) are labels; the particulars carry it.
- **Character pages show a choice.** A list of traits is received text. Give the one thing this character does that nobody else in the story would do.
- **Tone pages happen on the body.** Deliver what an audience will feel through particulars of sound, texture, pace and image, and let the reader name the feeling.
- **The world moves.** Show the setting as someone travels through it: the route to work, the market at closing time, the last ferry.
- **Horses across the deck.** A recurring image or object ties the pages into one story. In the deck it is a reading device; promise it on screen only when the creator has chosen it for the film.
- **The statement is head and heart.** A director's or creator's statement earns trust with specific craft choices (head) and, where they have offered one, a candid reason this story matters to them (heart), in their own voice. A clear interpretation and accountable choices can carry it on their own.

## Presenting work

- Open with the thumbnail: what you are showing, and the decision you need by when.
- Connect the work to the client's goals in the client's own words. That is head authority.
- Walk through the work in the order its audience will meet it. Let the client see why it works a beat before you say so.
- Name the risk yourself, with your plan for it. That is heart authority, and it makes your recommendation believable.
- Close on the decision: the options, your recommendation, the date. Then stop.

## Talks and speeches

- **Write for the ear.** Attribute each quotation as it begins. Keep subjects close to their verbs. End sentences on the word that matters.
- **Leave room.** After a laugh, a shock or a big line, give a beat (a short plain sentence, a pause, a returning phrase) before the next escalation. A room needs its laugh before the story can get worse.
- **Use a refrain.** A line that returns gives listeners a handhold and marks the passing of time.
- **Choose sounds that carry.** Hard consonants (P, K, T, D, B) pop into a microphone; runs of soft S, F and V sounds slacken.
- **Rehearse against a clock.** Read aloud and time it. Mark where breath runs out, where the room would laugh and where a turn comes too fast, then revise those places. Say whether the read was spoken or silent.
